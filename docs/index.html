01 / The question

Two tests for every country, every year

Is a country meeting the basic needs of its people? And is it doing so within a fair, equal-per-person share of the climate boundary?

The social foundation asks for life expectancy of at least 74 years, under-5 mortality of at most 25 per 1,000 births, electricity access for at least 95% of people, and employment for at least 94% of the labour force. The climate boundary divides the remaining 2 °C carbon budget equally among all people: 1.61 t of CO₂ per person per year, counted on a consumption basis so that emissions embodied in imports count where goods are used.

02 / The headline

Every country that crossed the social foundation did so while overshooting the climate boundary.

All 36 crossers. All 30 under a stricter three-year definition. With production-based emissions only Costa Rica and Sri Lanka appear safe and just, in 12 country-years, and both overshoot once imports are counted.

04 / Over three decades

More needs met. More overshoot.

The share of countries meeting the foundation grew, but always on the overshoot side of the boundary. The safe and just band stays empty in every year. Configurations are also sticky: 83% of countries stayed in the same configuration from one period to the next, and no country moved into the safe and just space.

05 / The carbon cost of development

Falling fast. Still five times too high.

The carbon requirement is the CO₂ per person at which half of countries meet the social foundation, estimated each year with a logistic model. It fell about 3.6% a year, from roughly 26 t in 1991 to 8.5 t in 2022. For the two health thresholds alone it fell to 2.6 t. It is a cross-country summary, not what any single country needs.

06 / Decoupling

Spreading. Not nearly fast enough.

Absolute decoupling, where income grows while consumption emissions fall, spread from 24 countries in 2001–2010 to 48 of 114 in 2011–2022. But among the 41 decouplers still above the boundary, the median cut was 1.6% a year against 5.5% needed to reach 1.61 t by 2050. Only Albania and the Dominican Republic are on track.

07 / What it means

Development has become far less carbon-intensive. Not yet fast enough.

Closing the gap needs emission cuts faster than any country has sustained, together with ways of providing basic needs that keep their carbon cost falling.

08 / The evidence

How it was done

  1. Build the panel

    155 countries with at least 1 million people, 1990–2022, from World Bank WDI, the Global Carbon Budget via Our World in Data, and Maddison PPP income. Interior gaps of up to three years are interpolated, never extrapolated.

  2. Classify every country-year

    Four thresholds and the 1.61 t boundary give four configurations. A missing indicator can never count as a threshold met.

  3. Estimate the carbon requirement

    A yearly logistic model of meeting the foundation on log CO₂ per person; its 50% point, with country-cluster bootstrap intervals and a pooled trend.

  4. Measure decoupling

    Trend growth of CO₂ and GDP per person by period, compared with the constant annual cut that reaches 1.61 t by 2050.

Robustness

The level of the requirement depends on how the foundation is defined. Its decline, and its position above the boundary, hold in every specification.

Limits

Read these before the numbers.

  • Descriptive, not causal

    All estimates are associations. The carbon requirement is a cross-country summary, not the emissions any single country needs.

  • One boundary, four indicators

    The study covers climate change only, not the other planetary boundaries, and four indicators do not capture every dimension of well-being. Employment is 100 minus the unemployment rate and misses informal and precarious work.

  • Strict thresholds

    The empty safe and just space depends on applying the thresholds strictly: with a 5% tolerance on each social threshold, El Salvador, Guatemala and Honduras reach it in 2022.

  • Uncertainty and scenarios

    Confidence intervals do not capture all measurement and model uncertainty. Equal-per-capita shares ignore historical responsibility. Projections to 2050 are illustrative, not forecasts.
